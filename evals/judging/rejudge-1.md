**Official guidance:** No-flush toilet: line a bucket or the bowl with heavy plastic bags, add disinfectant (diluted bleach) or absorbent (sawdust, kitty litter), seal and store bags; or pour a bucket of water into the bowl to flush if water is available; wash hands.

### Answer A
Sources it read: How to Unclog a Toilet; How to Turn Off the Water Supply to a Toilet

You have the water supply off (or no water coming into the toilet), and the toilet won't flush. Here’s what to do directly:

1. **Locate and turn off the toilet’s water supply valve** (if it exists). It’s usually a small knob or lever on the wall or floor behind the toilet, on the pipe leading to the tank. Turn it clockwise to shut off water. If you can’t find a valve (older toilet or main water off), skip to step 2.
2. **Flush the toilet to empty the tank and bowl**. Pull or press the flush handle down (or lift the tank lid and pour out remaining water if the handle doesn’t work). This removes water from the bowl so you can inspect it.
3. **Check the flush mechanism**:  
   - Look inside the tank. Is the flapper (the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank) covering the hole? If it’s stuck down or the chain is tangled, the bowl won’t fill when you flush.  
   - If the flapper is loose or the chain is too long/short, adjust it so the flapper can drop fully when you flush.
4. **Try flushing again**. 5. **If you need to remove waste**: Use a plunger (if the bowl has some water) or a bucket to scoop out waste. Do not flush again until water supply is restored.
6. **Restore water (if available)**: Turn the supply valve counterclockwise to reopen. Wait a few seconds, then flush—the tank should refill and the bowl should work if the flapper/chain is correct.

**Important**: If the main house water is off (e.g., utility shut-off, outage), you cannot get water into the toilet until the main supply is restored. You can only empty the bowl manually. Do not try to force water in; wait for supply to return or use bucket flush if needed.

## Question 17: Is it safe to take ibuprofen that expired two years ago?

**Official guidance:** Expired ibuprofen: usually safe but may be less effective; the FDA recommends not using expired medicine; ask a pharmacist when possible.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Drug expiration; How and when to get rid of unused medicines; Prescription drug

No, it is not safe to take ibuprofen that expired two years ago. The expiration date on medication is the final day the manufacturer guarantees full potency and safety. While some drugs may remain effective for a time past expiration (studies show some can work for months or even years in ideal storage), the manufacturer cannot guarantee safety or effectiveness after expiration. Using expired medication risks reduced effectiveness (may not relieve pain properly) and potential safety issues (unknown stability, possible contamination or chemical changes).

## Question 18: How do I make oral rehydration solution at home?

**Official guidance:** WHO: 1 liter of clean water + 6 level teaspoons of sugar + half a level teaspoon of salt.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Oral rehydration therapy; Reduced concentration oral rehydration solution; How to Make an Oral Rehydration Salts Drink (ORS)

The safest, simplest homemade oral rehydration solution (ORS) for dehydration from diarrhea or vomiting uses clean water, sugar, and salt in a specific ratio.

**Direct answer:**  
Mix **40 grams (about 4 large tablespoons or 8 level teaspoons) of sugar** and **3.5 grams (about ½ small teaspoon or a level ½‑teaspoon) of table salt** into **1 liter (about 4 cups) of clean drinking water**. Stir well until dissolved. Drink this slowly.

**Why this ratio:**  
- WHO/UNICEF’s 2004 home‑made ORS recipe calls for 3.5 g salt and 40 g sugar per liter of water.  
- Appropedia’s “simplest version” also uses 1 level teaspoon salt and 8 level teaspoons sugar per liter. Both match the WHO guideline when measured properly (≈3.5 g salt ≈½ small spoon, ≈40 g sugar ≈4 big spoons).

**Short numbered steps:**  
1. Wash hands with soap and water.  
2. Measure 1 liter of clean water (e.g., bottled water or boiled‑then‑cooled water; do not use milk, juice, or soft drinks).  
3. Add ½ small spoon (≈3.5 g) of table salt (iodized or kosher salt is fine; avoid excess iodine if possible).  
4. Add 4 big spoons (≈40 g) of granulated sugar (or equivalent; if using level teaspoons, that’s about 8 level teaspoons).  
5. Stir vigorously until the sugar and salt are completely dissolved.  
6. Drink the solution slowly over the next few hours as needed. It can be refrigerated for up to 24 hours; do not store longer.

**Safety notes:**  
- Use only clean water (boiled and cooled if tap water might be contaminated).  
- Do not add extra salt or sugar beyond the measured amounts; too much salt can worsen dehydration, too much sugar can draw more water into the gut and cause diarrhea.  
- If the person is a young child, an infant, or unable to swallow/drink well, use smaller frequent sips (e.g., 1–2 oz every few minutes) and monitor for improved urine output and reduced thirst.  
- If symptoms worsen (e.g., persistent vomiting, blood in stool, severe abdominal pain, signs of shock), seek medical care immediately; call emergency services (e.g., 911) if needed.

**Sources:**  
- Appropedia “Oral rehydration therapy”: simplest version = 1 level teaspoon salt + 8 level teaspoons sugar per liter; WHO/UNICEF 2004 home‑made ORS = 3.5 g salt + 40 g sugar per liter.  
- Appropedia “Reduced concentration oral rehydration solution”: WHO recipe = ½ small spoon salt (3.5 g) + 4 big spoons sugar (40 g) per liter.  
- WikiHow “How to Make an Oral Rehydration Salts Drink (ORS)”: recommends ½‑teaspoon salt + 2 tablespoons sugar per liter (consistent with the WHO ratio when measured accurately).  

*(Sources: Appropedia articles 1274280 and 1294997; WikiHow article 69075.)*

## Question 20: How can I tell if home-canned food might have botulism?

**Official guidance:** Possible botulism: bulging, leaking or swollen containers, spurting liquid, foam, cloudy liquid, off smell. Never taste; discard safely (sealed bags), clean spills with bleach; get medical care for symptoms.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Botulism; Botulism; Botulism

You cannot reliably tell from sight, smell, or taste alone whether home-canned food contains the botulism toxin — **the toxin itself is odorless, tasteless, and colorless, and bacteria may grow without causing visible changes in the food**. However, there are clear safety warning signs that should cause you to discard the food immediately:

1. **Any home-canned food in a metal can that bulges outward** (the lid pushes against the side of the can or the can looks deformed).  
2. **Food that smells bad, looks foamy, or has an unusual appearance** inside the can.  
3. **Any sign the seal is broken, damaged, or not truly airtight** (e.g., rust holes, dents that compromise the seal).

If you see any of these signs (bulging, bad odor/foam, damaged seal), **throw the food away — do not taste or eat it**.

If the can looks normal (no bulging, no bad smell, seal intact), you cannot safely confirm it is free of toxin just by looking or smelling. The safest action is to **discard home-canned foods that were not processed under proper pressure-canning guidelines** (e.g., pressure cooked at ≥ 250°F / 121°C for the recommended time for that food), especially low-acid foods like vegetables, meats, beans, and home-cooked sauces. When in doubt, throw it away.

**If you eat such food and develop any of these symptoms within 12–36 hours (typical for foodborne botulism), call 911 or go to the emergency room immediately:**
- Double or blurred vision
- Drooping eyelids
- Slurred speech or difficulty speaking
- Difficulty swallowing
- Dry mouth
- Muscle weakness (often starting in the face, arms, or legs)
- Nausea and vomiting

These symptoms are medical emergencies; botulism can cause paralysis and breathing failure.

**Bottom line:** Only discard home-canned food if you see bulging, bad odor/foam, or a damaged seal. If the can looks normal but you are unsure about proper pressure-canning steps, **do not risk eating it — throw it away**. When uncertain, always discard.

Sources: MedlinePlus (NIH) – "Always throw away bulging cans or foul-smelling preserved foods." WikiMed (Wikipedia) – "Metal cans containing food in which bacteria are growing may bulge outwards... cans with any of these signs should be discarded."

(Note: The library sources do not provide a simple checklist for consumers to test canned food for botulism; they emphasize discarding visibly abnormal cans and using proper pressure canning. No home test (e.g., boiling, smelling alone) is reliable for detecting the toxin.)

### Answer B
Sources it read: Botulism; Botulism; Botulism

**How to tell if home‑canned food might have botulism**

1. **Look at the container** – Discard any can or jar that is:
   - Bulging, swollen, or visibly distended  
   - Leaking, cracked, dented, or otherwise damaged  
   - Has a lid that “pops” or won’t stay sealed  

2. **Smell and inspect the food** – When you open the container, do not taste it.  
   - If the food has a foul, sour, or “off” odor, or appears foamy, cloudy, or has visible mold, discard it.  
   - Remember: botulism toxin itself is odorless and tasteless, so normal‑looking food can still be dangerous.

3. **Check for symptoms after eating** – If anyone who ate the food develops any of the classic adult symptoms, seek emergency care immediately:
   - Double vision, blurred vision, or trouble focusing  
   - Dry mouth, difficulty speaking or swallowing  
   - Muscle weakness that starts at the head/neck and spreads downward  
   - Respiratory difficulty or shortness of breath  
   - No fever is expected.  
   Symptoms usually appear 8–36 hours after ingestion, but can be as early as 6 hours or as late as 48 hours.

4. **When in doubt, throw it out** – If you have any doubt about the safety of a home‑canned item, do not eat it. The risk of severe illness or death outweighs any chance of saving the food.

**Safety actions**
- If you or someone else shows any of the neurologic symptoms listed above, call 911 (or your local emergency number) and go to the nearest emergency department right away.  
- Do not give honey or corn syrup to infants under 1 year old, and always pressure‑cook home‑canned low‑acid foods at 250 °F (121 °C) for at least 30 minutes to kill any botulinum toxin.
