## Question 1: Can I run my gas generator in the garage if I leave the big door open?

**Official guidance:** Never run a generator in a garage, even with the door open (CO). Outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the house, away from windows, doors and vents. Have battery CO alarms.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Power Outages

**No — never run a generator in the garage, even with the door open.**

Carbon monoxide (CO) is colorless and odorless and can kill you, your family, and your pets. It can build up and drift into your home through the garage even when the door is open.

**What to do instead:**

1. Place the generator **outdoors**, at least **20 feet away** from windows, doors, and any attached garage.
2. Keep it dry and out of rain/flood water (wet = shock hazard).
3. Use heavy-duty extension cords to connect appliances.
4. Let it cool before refueling (hot engine + spilled fuel = fire).
5. Make sure you have working CO detectors on every level of your home.

If anyone in your home develops headache, dizziness, nausea, or confusion during a power outage, get outside to fresh air immediately and call 911.

## Question 2: The tap water might be contaminated. How many drops of household bleach per gallon make it safe?

**Official guidance:** EPA: plain unscented bleach, 6%: 8 drops (about 1/8 tsp) per gallon; 8.25%: 6 drops. Double for cloudy water (filter first). Stir, wait 30 minutes; a slight chlorine smell means it worked. Older FEMA guidance says 16 drops; both are acceptable if consistent.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Are You Ready?/Shelter from Natural Disasters; How to Clean Beer Bottles; Water Disinfection | CDC Yellow Book 2024

The safe household bleach dosage for disinfecting tap water (when contamination is microbial/bacterial/viral and no chemical contamination is suspected) is:

- **Use only regular household liquid bleach that contains 5.25% to 6.0% sodium hypochlorite** (no scented, color-safe, or cleaner-added bleaches).  
- **Add 16 drops (1/8 teaspoon) of bleach per gallon of water**, stir well, and let stand for **30 minutes**. The water should have a slight chlorine/bleach odor.  
- If the water does **not** smell of chlorine after 30 minutes, repeat the same 16 drops per gallon and let stand another **15 minutes**.  
- If it still does **not** smell of chlorine after the second dose/wait, **discard the water and find another safe source** (e.g., bottled water, boiled water from a clean source).

**Important safety notes:**  
- Bleach does **not** remove chemical contaminants (e.g., heavy metals, industrial pollutants). If you suspect chemical contamination (e.g., from a broken pipe, industrial spill, or unknown source), **do not drink or treat the water with bleach—seek bottled water or boil clean water only**. Boiling kills microbes but does not remove chemicals.  
- Never use bleach if the water is known to be chemically contaminated.  
- Always check the bleach label for sodium hypochlorite percentage; use a newly opened or unopened bottle (potency decreases over time).  
- Do not mix bleach with other cleaners or chemicals (risk of toxic gas).  
- If in doubt, or if symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea) occur after drinking treated water, seek medical help immediately.

**Why not "drops per gallon" as a fixed number without timing?** The 16 drops (1/8 tsp) per gallon is the standard dose for a 30-minute contact time to reliably kill bacteria and viruses. The extra 15-minute check after a second dose ensures proper disinfection. Timing matters because bleach potency decreases over time.

## Signs of Carbon Monoxide (CO) Poisoning

CO is odorless, colorless, and tasteless — you can't detect it without a detector. Symptoms mimic the flu or food poisoning:

- **Mild:** Dull, frontal headache (most common), dizziness, nausea, fatigue, weakness
- **Moderate:** Confusion, blurred vision, fast heart rate, vomiting

5. **Do not re-enter the building** until fire/emergency services confirm it's safe.
6. After an incident, have the furnace, water heater, stove, or any fuel-burning appliance inspected before use.

## Question 5: Someone is shivering hard and acting confused after being out in the cold. What do I do?

**Official guidance:** Likely hypothermia: call 911; move somewhere warm; remove wet clothing; warm the core first (chest, neck, head, groin) with dry blankets or skin-to-skin; warm sweet non-alcoholic drinks only if alert; don't rub limbs; handle gently; CPR if not breathing.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Hypothermia; Accidental hypothermia; Hypothermia; Do emergency space blankets actually work?

You are describing signs of hypothermia. Act quickly:

1. **Call emergency services (911 or local emergency number) immediately.** Hypothermia can progress rapidly, especially with confusion and shivering.
2. **Move the person to a warm, dry place** if safe to do so (e.g., indoors, away from wind and wet ground). Cover head, neck, and extremities with dry clothing or blankets.
3. **Do not give them alcohol, caffeine, or hot drinks** (they can worsen confusion or cause burns).
4. **Warm their core slowly** using dry blankets or warm clothing. Do not use direct heat (hot water, heating pads, or direct flames).
5. **If they are conscious and able to swallow, offer warm (not hot) non-alcoholic fluids** (e.g., warm water with a little sugar). Avoid forcing if they are confused or vomiting.
6. **Monitor closely** for signs of improvement (e.g., reduced shivering, clearer thinking, normal breathing). If confusion worsens or they become unresponsive, begin CPR if trained, and keep calling emergency services.

Do not delay—hypothermia can lead to cardiac arrest or death quickly. Even mild hypothermia requires prompt warming.

## Question 6: Someone cut their arm badly and it is bleeding a lot. What do I do?

**Official guidance:** Severe bleeding: call 911; firm direct pressure with a clean cloth; if blood soaks through, add more on top without removing; tourniquet above the wound for life-threatening limb bleeding if pressure fails.

**Directly answer the question:**  
Apply firm, continuous direct pressure directly to the wound with a clean cloth, gauze, or your hand. Keep pressing until the bleeding stops—this may take several minutes. If possible, elevate the arm above heart level. Do not remove a soaked dressing; add another layer if needed. If bleeding does not stop after 5–10 minutes of firm direct pressure (especially if it’s heavy/gushing), consider using a tourniquet only as a last resort for life‑threatening bleeding (e.g., arterial spurting). Otherwise, keep pressure and elevate.

**Short numbered steps:**  
1. Clean your hands (or wear gloves if available).  
2. Place a clean, thick cloth, gauze, or your hand directly over the bleeding wound.  
3. Press firmly and continuously; do not peek or lift the dressing. Keep pressure on for as long as needed—usually 5–20 minutes—until bleeding slows and stops.  
4. If the wound is on an arm or leg, gently elevate the limb above heart level to help reduce bleeding.  
5. If bleeding continues heavily (e.g., bright red, spurting) after firm pressure for a few minutes, and you have no other option, apply a tourniquet (a tight band above the wound, not over it) only as a last resort. Note the time you applied it and seek professional medical care immediately.  
6. Once bleeding stops, cover the wound with a clean dressing and monitor for signs of shock (pale, sweaty, weak pulse, confusion). If shock is suspected, lay the person flat, raise their legs slightly, and keep them warm.

- Call emergency services (e.g., 911) if the bleeding is heavy, does not stop with direct pressure, or if the person shows signs of shock.

Sources it read: Burn; Burn

**Immediate action for a burned hand from a stove**

1. **Cool the burn** – Run cool (not cold) tap water on the burned area for 10–20 minutes, or use a clean, cool compress. Do not use ice, because it can worsen tissue damage.  
2. **Protect the burn** – After cooling, gently clean the skin with mild soap and water, then cover with a sterile, non‑adhesive dressing or a clean, soft cloth.  
3. **Pain & inflammation** – If you have an over‑the‑counter pain reliever (e.g., acetaminophen or ibuprofen) and it’s appropriate for you, take it as directed.  
4. **Watch for complications** – Seek medical care (or call emergency services) if you notice:  
   - The burn is deep (blistered, oozing, or shows deep red/black tissue).  
   - The burn is large (more than a quarter‑inch or covers a joint).  
   - You have difficulty moving the hand, severe swelling, redness spreading rapidly, or signs of infection (increased pain, redness, warmth, pus).  
   - You are unable to keep the burn clean or protected, or you have underlying health conditions that make burns more serious.  
5. **When to get professional help** – Any burn that looks severe, is large, deep, or you cannot manage at home should be evaluated by a healthcare professional (burn center, urgent care, or emergency room).  

**General safety reminder** – Burns can cause hidden damage; always follow up with a medical professional if the burn is more than a small, superficial burn.

## Question 8: The power is out. How long can my insulin stay unrefrigerated?

**Official guidance:** Insulin: opened vials/pens are usually fine at room temperature (below about 86°F/30°C) for 28 days (some types 42-56); unopened can also be kept at room temperature up to the same limit. Keep out of heat and don't freeze. Check the label.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Insulin and syringes - storage and safety; Insulin Lispro Injection; Giving an insulin injection

If your insulin is already opened (you’ve taken some doses from the vial, pen, or cartridge), it can be kept at **room temperature (59°F to 86°F / 15°C to 30°C)** for up to **28 days**. After 28 days, discard it, even if it looks fine.

If your insulin is **unopened** (still in its original container/vial/pen), keep it in the refrigerator (36°F to 46°F / 2°C to 8°C) until the expiration date on the label. Do **not** freeze it.

**Important:**  
- If the power outage lasts longer than 48–72 hours and you cannot keep the insulin refrigerated (e.g., no working fridge), follow the room-temperature limit of 28 days for opened insulin.  
- Discard insulin that has been frozen or exposed to temperatures above 98.6°F (37°C).  
- Always check the expiration date; never use insulin past its "use-by" date.  
- If you’re unsure about your specific insulin type or storage instructions, check the label or contact your pharmacist/doctor.

**Sources:** MedlinePlus "Insulin and syringes - storage and safety" (id 2498286), MedlinePlus "Giving an insulin injection" (id 2495722), MedlinePlus "Insulin Lispro Injection" (id 2450345).

## Question 9: Is it safe to use candles for light during a blackout?

**Official guidance:** Ready.gov: use flashlights or battery lanterns, not candles. If candles are used: never unattended, away from flammables, on a stable holder.

### Answer A
Sources it read: How to Decorate Safely for the Holiday Season; Power Outages

It is generally considered unsafe to use candles for light during a blackout due to the high risk of accidental fires. It is much safer to use flashlights or battery-powered lanterns. However, if you choose to use candles, follow these safety steps to minimize the risk:

1. **Use Stable Holders:** Place candles only in stable holders on flat surfaces where they cannot be easily knocked over.
2. **Keep Away from Flammables:** Keep lit candles far away from anything flammable, such as curtains, bedding, paper, or decorations.
3. **Never Leave Unattended:** Always extinguish a candle before leaving the room or going to sleep. Do not leave the house with candles burning.
4. **Secure the Area:** Keep candles out of reach of children and pets.

**Safer Alternatives:**
*   Use flashlights with extra batteries.
*   Use battery-operated rechargeable lanterns or headlamps.

Sources: Ready.gov (FEMA), wikiHow

### Answer B
Sources it read: Fire safety at home

Yes, you can use candles for light during a blackout, **but only if you follow strict safety rules**. Candles are a fire hazard and must never be left unattended.

**Key safety steps:**
1. Place candles on a stable, heat-resistant surface away from anything that can burn (curtains, furniture, papers, etc.). Keep at least 12 inches (30 cm) clear.
2. Never leave a burning candle unattended. Extinguish it before leaving the room or going to sleep.
3. Keep candles out of reach of children and pets.
4. Use flameless (LED) candles if possible—they provide the same light without fire risk.
5. Ensure smoke alarms are working; candles can start fires that spread quickly.
6. Do not use candles in enclosed spaces without ventilation; though they don’t produce carbon monoxide like generators, they still pose a fire risk.

**If a candle starts a fire, evacuate immediately and call 911.**

Sources: MedlinePlus (NIH) Fire safety at home, Ready.gov Home Fires.

## Question 10: My phone is at 15% and the power is out. How do I make the battery last?

**Official guidance:** Phone: Low Power Mode / battery saver; lower brightness; close apps; turn off radios or use airplane mode when not needed; text rather than call; charge from a power bank or a car running outdoors.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Power Outages; How to Conserve Battery Life on a Windows Phone; How to Save Battery Life in iOS 101

Direct answer: With your phone at 15% and power out, use these simple steps to stretch the battery as long as possible:

1. **Turn on Low Power Mode** (if your phone has it). This reduces screen brightness, background activity, and automatic updates.  
2. **Dim the screen brightness** manually to the lowest comfortable level; avoid auto-brightness if possible.  
3. **Switch to Airplane Mode** and disable Wi‑Fi, cellular data, and Bluetooth. This stops the phone from searching for networks and using radio power.  
4. **Close all apps you’re not using** and avoid opening maps, videos, games, or streaming. Keep the phone locked when not in use.  
5. **Don’t charge the phone** unless you have a safe, reliable power source (e.g., a wall outlet or a known good power bank); charging at low battery won’t hurt but using power unnecessarily drains it faster.  
6. **Use the phone only for essential tasks** (calls, texts, simple notes). Avoid background sync, email fetch, location services, and screen animations.  
7. **Place the phone face down** when not actively using it; the screen stays off, saving power.

These steps are based on Ready.gov guidance (general power‑outage preparedness) and wikiHow tips on dimming brightness and using Low Power / Airplane Mode.

Sources: Ready.gov (Power Outages), wikiHow (Conserve Battery Life on a Windows Phone), wikiHow (Save Battery Life in iOS 101)
