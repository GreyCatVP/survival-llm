## Question 0: The power just went out. How long will the food in my fridge and freezer stay safe?

**Official guidance:** USDA: a closed fridge keeps food safe about 4 hours; a full freezer about 48 hours, half-full about 24. Discard perishables above 40°F (4°C) for 2+ hours. Never taste to check.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Food

According to FEMA/Ready.gov:

- **Refrigerator:** about **4 hours** if you keep the door closed.
- **Full freezer:** about **48 hours** if the door stays closed.
- **Half-full freezer:** about **24 hours** if the door stays closed.

**What to do now:**
1. Keep both doors closed as much as possible.
2. After power returns, check the temperature. Refrigerated food is safe only if it stayed at or below **40°F (4°C)**.
3. Throw out any perishable food (meat, poultry, fish, eggs, leftovers) that has been above 40°F for **2 hours or more**. When in doubt, throw it out.

Sources: Food (Ready.gov (FEMA))

## Question 1: Can I run my gas generator in the garage if I leave the big door open?

**Official guidance:** Never run a generator in a garage, even with the door open (CO). Outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the house, away from windows, doors and vents. Have battery CO alarms.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Power Outages

No, you should not run your gas generator in the garage—even with the big door open. Carbon monoxide (CO) is a colorless, odorless, deadly gas that can build up quickly in enclosed or semi-enclosed spaces like a garage, even with an opening. Ready.gov (FEMA) explicitly states: "Generators and fuel should always be used outdoors and at least 20 feet away from windows, doors and attached garages."

**Why leaving the door open is still unsafe:**  
A garage is partially enclosed; airflow through an open door may not be sufficient to prevent CO buildup, especially if doors or windows to the house are closed or blocked. CO can seep into your home and affect anyone inside.

**Safe alternatives:**  
- Run the generator **outside**, at least 20 feet away from windows, doors, and the garage entrance.  
- Keep the area well-ventilated (e.g., place it away from walls/facades that block airflow).  
- Install and test a working carbon monoxide detector on every level of your home.  
- Never operate the generator indoors, in a basement, or in any enclosed space.

If you need power during an outage, use the generator outdoors only, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Sources: Ready.gov (FEMA) Generator Safety.

## Question 2: The tap water might be contaminated. How many drops of household bleach per gallon make it safe?

**Official guidance:** EPA: plain unscented bleach, 6%: 8 drops (about 1/8 tsp) per gallon; 8.25%: 6 drops. Double for cloudy water (filter first). Stir, wait 30 minutes; a slight chlorine smell means it worked. Older FEMA guidance says 16 drops; both are acceptable if consistent.

### Answer A
Sources it read: Water

**8 drops of regular household liquid bleach per gallon** (this equals 1/8 teaspoon, the Ready.gov dose).

Steps:
1. Use only regular, unscented household liquid bleach with **5.25–6.0% sodium hypochlorite** as the only active ingredient. No "color-safe," scented, or "plus" varieties.
2. Add **8 drops (1/8 tsp) per gallon**, stir, and let stand **30 minutes**.
3. It should have a **slight** chlorine smell. If not, add another 8 drops, wait 15 more minutes. If it still doesn't smell of chlorine, **discard it** and find another source.

**Important warnings:**
- Bleach kills **microbes only**. It does **not** remove chemicals, heavy metals, or salts. If the contamination is chemical (e.g., industrial spill, fuel), bleach will not make it safe — you'd need distillation or another source.
- If water is cloudy, filter it first (coffee filter, cloth) before adding bleach.
- Boiling is a reliable alternative: bring to a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 ft elevation).

Sources: Ready.gov (FEMA) – Water, Chlorination section.

### Answer B
Sources it read: Water Purification :: Washington State Dept. of Health [Water (2)]

To make water safe to drink by boiling, bring it to a **rolling boil** (where bubbles are actively breaking the surface) for **at least 1 minute**. After boiling, let the water cool before drinking.

The direct answer: At sea level, bring water to a **rolling boil for 1 full minute** to make it safe to drink from a potentially contaminated source (e.g., natural water, wells, after disasters). At higher altitudes, add **1 minute for every additional 1,000 meters (about 3,300 feet)** above sea level. For example, at 2,000 meters (about 6,600 feet), boil for 3 minutes.

**Short numbered steps:**
1. Bring water to a rolling (vigorous) boil.
2. Maintain the boil for the required time:
   - Sea level: 1 minute
   - Every 1,000 m (3,300 ft) above sea level: add 1 minute
3. Let water cool before drinking.

**Safety note:** Boiling kills most pathogens (bacteria, viruses, protozoa) but does not remove chemical pollutants (e.g., heavy metals, pesticides). If water is known to contain chemicals, additional filtration or treatment is needed. Do not drink water that is only heated (not boiled) or boiled for less than the recommended time at altitude.

## Question 4: What are the signs of carbon monoxide poisoning and what should I do?

**Official guidance:** CO poisoning: headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea/vomiting, chest pain, confusion. Get everyone into fresh air at once, call 911, don't go back in.

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a colorless and odorless gas, which makes it extremely dangerous because you cannot detect it by sight or smell. It replaces the oxygen in your bloodstream, causing your heart, brain, and body to be starved of oxygen (hypoxia).

**Signs and Symptoms can include:**

*   Headache (very common)
*   Dizziness
*   Fatigue
*   Nausea or vomiting
*   Malaise (a general feeling of discomfort)
*   Confusion
*   Sleepiness
*   In severe cases, loss of consciousness (LOC) or coma.

These symptoms are often mistaken for common illnesses like the flu.

**What to Do (Safety First):**

1.  **Get to Fresh Air Immediately:** If you suspect CO poisoning, move yourself and anyone else away from the source of the gas and into fresh air immediately.
2.  **Call Emergency Services:** Seek emergency medical attention right away.
3.  **Do Not Re-enter:** Do not go back into the area until it has been properly ventilated and deemed safe by emergency responders.

**Danger Warning:** Carbon monoxide poisoning can cause sudden illness and death. **If you or someone near you is experiencing symptoms, this is a medical emergency, and you must call 911 or your local emergency number immediately.**

3. **If oxygen is available**, administer it
4. **For severe cases:** The victim may require rescue breathing—call emergency services immediately
5. **Prevention:** Ensure adequate ventilation whenever using a fire or fuel-burning appliance near your shelter

Here’s what to do if someone is shivering hard and acting confused after being in the cold (possible hypothermia):

1. **Call emergency services immediately** (e.g., 911) if they are unconscious, have severe confusion, or you suspect severe hypothermia. Do not wait—hypothermia can progress quickly and cause life‑threatening complications.

2. **Keep them warm and dry**  
   - Remove any wet clothing (wet clothes cause heat loss).  
   - Cover their head, neck, and hands with dry blankets or clothing.  
   - If you have a space blanket or emergency blanket, place it under dry layers (not directly on skin).  
   - If possible, move them to a warm room, insulated shelter, or a heated vehicle.

3. **Warm them gradually**  
   - Do not use direct heat sources (hot water baths, heating pads, direct flames) as they can cause burns or shock.  
   - If they are alert and can swallow, give warm (not hot) fluids (e.g., warm water, warm tea without caffeine) in small sips.  
   - Use gentle, indirect warmth: place blankets around them, or have them sit near a heat source (e.g., fireplace, heater) while staying dry.

4. **Monitor and prepare for possible medical help**  
   - Check breathing, pulse, and level of consciousness every few minutes.  
   - If they become unresponsive, begin CPR only if you are trained and the person has no pulse or breathing.  
   - Keep them still and avoid moving them if possible; moving can worsen hypothermia.  
   - If you have a thermometer, note their core temperature (if possible) but focus on keeping them warm.

5. **Do NOT**  
   - Give them alcohol, caffeine, or sugary drinks (they can worsen confusion).  
   - Use direct heat (hot water, heating pads, lamps) which can cause burns or shock.  
   - Let them shiver excessively—shivering actually generates heat, but if they are too cold, stopping shivering may lead to further cooling.

If you cannot get professional help right away, continue to keep them warm, dry, and monitor closely. The sooner you get professional medical assistance, the better the outcome.
